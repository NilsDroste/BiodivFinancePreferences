**Cover Letter — Nature Sustainability**

Dear Editors,

We submit for your consideration as an **Article**, "Mandatory biodiversity
financing is preferred even where voluntary should work," by Nils Droste, Jens
Marl Christiansen and Yohei Mitani.

**The assumption we test.** The Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework
commits governments to mobilising at least USD 200 billion per year for nature by
2030, and leans heavily on voluntary, market-based and non-state finance to do
it. Biodiversity credits, corporate pledges and certification schemes are being
built out on that premise. A substantial evidence base now asks whether such
instruments can deliver, and finds that they struggle to scale, but that work is
uniformly supply-side. Voluntary architecture also presupposes something on the
demand side: that citizens are willing to route conservation money through
voluntary channels rather than requiring the state to compel it. That
presupposition has not been tested directly, and it is not a safe one to leave
untested beneath a strategy of this size.

**What we find.** Using a preregistered discrete choice experiment (n = 2,101)
combined with an incentivised donation experiment, we find that Swedish citizens
consistently prefer mandatory biodiversity financing. Public monitoring commands
a willingness-to-pay premium of approximately USD 160 per household per year (95%
CI 127 to 195), and donation-financed programmes are valued some USD 250 below an
otherwise identical tax-financed one. The standard political-economy objection
fails on its own terms: strategic free-riding predicts the strongest voluntary
preference among high-concern non-donors, and we observe the weakest. The
citizens who care most, and who give their own money under incentive, are the
most opposed to voluntary financing.

**Why the single-country design carries beyond Sweden.** The test is demanding by
construction. A most-likely case requires two conditions to hold jointly:
voluntary provision must be feasible, and there must be an unmet need for it to
fill. Sweden ranks fifth of 91 countries worldwide on interpersonal trust, so
citizens have little reason to doubt that others would contribute, while its
public biodiversity spending is among the lowest in Europe, so the state cannot
be seen as already covering the problem. Sweden is close to the only country in
Europe satisfying both. Those two conditions close off the leading alternative
explanations for a preference for compulsion, which is what makes a null result
here informative well beyond the case itself.

**Why Nature Sustainability.** The finding bears directly on how a major
international financing architecture is being built, and it cuts against the
prevailing justification for it. Voluntary biodiversity finance is often defended
as the pragmatic option, on the assumption that public appetite for regulation is
limited. Our evidence indicates the binding constraint is not citizen
unwillingness to be governed or to pay. We also surface a tension your readers
will recognise: the instrument citizens most prefer, mandatory corporate
offsetting, is the one with the largest evidence base for ecological failure in
practice. We read this as demand for mandatory corporate accountability rather
than endorsement of offsetting markets as currently operated, which sharpens the
governance question rather than settling it.

**Declarations.** The study was preregistered before data collection (AsPredicted
#273748 and #287153), and we state explicitly which analyses those registrations
cover and which are exploratory. Anonymized microdata and full replication code
are already publicly deposited (doi:10.5281/zenodo.22113029); both the manuscript
and supplementary information render from the deposited data without access to
restricted files. The study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority
(2025-04420-01) and all participants gave informed consent. The manuscript is not
under consideration elsewhere, all authors approve this submission, and we
declare no competing interests.

We suggest reviewers with expertise in biodiversity finance and governance,
stated preference methods, and the political economy of environmental policy
instruments.
