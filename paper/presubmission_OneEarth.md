## Title

Citizens prefer mandatory over voluntary biodiversity financing even under
favorable conditions

## Authors

Nils Droste (Department of Food and Resource Economics, University of
Copenhagen), Jens Marl Christiansen (Department of People and Technology,
Roskilde University), Yohei Mitani (Graduate School of Global Environmental
Studies, Kyoto University). Corresponding author: nd@ifro.ku.dk

## Abstract

Global biodiversity governance has shifted substantially toward voluntary,
market-based and non-state financing instruments, but whether citizens support
these over mandatory alternatives remains largely unknown. We test this in
Sweden, a most-likely case for voluntary instrument support: it ranks fifth of 91
countries worldwide on interpersonal trust, while public spending on biodiversity
protection sits in the bottom tercile in Europe, so voluntary provision should be
both feasible and needed. Using a preregistered discrete choice experiment
(n = 2,101) combined with an incentivised donation experiment, we find that
Swedish citizens consistently prefer mandatory biodiversity financing
instruments. Mandatory corporate offsetting carries the highest mean utility;
public monitoring commands a willingness-to-pay premium of approximately USD 160
per household per year. Citizens who care most about biodiversity and personally
contribute to conservation prefer mandatory instruments most strongly, a pattern
inconsistent with strategic free-riding. Respondents also rated mandatory
instruments more effective at delivering biodiversity benefits, and this
perceived relative effectiveness predicts lower voluntary preference. Voluntary
donation lacks a natural constituency. These findings bear directly on GBF Target
19 implementation: even where conditions most favour voluntary provision,
building mandatory regulatory infrastructure is more closely aligned with citizen
support.

## Why this is significant and broadly interesting

**Context.** The Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework commits
governments to mobilising at least USD 200 billion per year for nature by 2030,
and leans heavily on voluntary, market-based and non-state finance to do it.
Biodiversity credits, corporate pledges, certification schemes and philanthropic
giving are all being built out on that premise across Europe and beyond.

**Rationale.** A growing evidence base asks whether these instruments can deliver
and finds that they struggle to scale. That work is uniformly supply-side: can
private capital be mobilised, can markets hold integrity, can non-state actors
commit credibly. The prior question has not been asked. Voluntary financing
architecture presupposes that citizens are willing to route conservation money
through voluntary channels rather than demanding the state compel it. Nobody has
tested that presupposition directly, and it is not a safe assumption to leave
untested when it underpins a USD 200 billion strategy.

**Approach.** We test it where it should hold most easily. A most-likely case
needs two conditions to co-occur: voluntary provision must be feasible, and there
must be an unmet need for it to fill. Sweden is close to the only country in
Europe satisfying both, combining top-tercile interpersonal trust with
bottom-tercile public biodiversity spending. Citizens there have little reason to
doubt that others would contribute, and cannot regard the state as already
covering the problem. A null result in that setting is therefore informative well
beyond Sweden, because it closes off the two leading alternative explanations for
a preference for compulsion.

**Significance.** Citizens reject voluntary financing decisively, and they do so
for reasons that make the result harder to dismiss. The standard political
economy objection holds that apparent support for compulsion is strategic:
high-concern citizens endorsing mandates so that others pay. That hypothesis
predicts the strongest voluntary preference among people who care about
biodiversity but do not give. We observe the weakest. The citizens who care most,
and who give their own money in an incentivised allocation, are the most opposed
to voluntary financing. What they report instead is a judgement about
effectiveness: mandatory instruments are rated more likely to deliver
biodiversity benefits, and that judgement tracks their choices.

**Implications and societal relevance.** Voluntary biodiversity finance is
frequently defended as the pragmatic option, on the assumption that public
appetite for regulation is limited. Our evidence points the other way: in the
context most favourable to voluntary provision, the binding constraint is not
citizen unwillingness to be governed or to pay. That matters for how governments
read the politics of biodiversity policy, and for whether the current
architecture is building on a foundation its own beneficiaries would choose. The
paper also supplies a lower-bound benchmark and testable cross-national
hypotheses for comparative work, including the possibility that voluntary
instrument preference, where it appears elsewhere, reflects a pragmatic response
to weak state institutions rather than genuine endorsement.
