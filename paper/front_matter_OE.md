# Highlights

- Swedes prefer mandatory over voluntary biodiversity financing instruments
- Public monitoring commands a willingness-to-pay premium of USD 160 per year
- The most committed and most generous citizens are the most anti-voluntary
- Distrust of the state, not doubt it delivers, drives voluntary preference

# eTOC blurb

Global biodiversity finance leans on voluntary instruments, which presumes
citizens would rather give than be required to pay. Droste et al. test that in
Sweden, where voluntary provision should work best, and find the opposite:
citizens consistently choose mandatory financing, most strongly among those who
care most and give most. Distrust of the state, rather than doubt that it
delivers, is what shifts anyone toward voluntary channels.

# Science for society

Nature is in decline, and governments have promised to spend at least USD 200
billion a year to slow it. Much of that money is meant to come from voluntary
sources: companies buying biodiversity credits, shoppers paying more for
certified goods, people donating to conservation charities. This approach assumes
citizens would rather give than be taxed, or have companies required to pay. We
tested that assumption in Sweden, a country where voluntary giving should work
especially well, because people trust each other unusually highly and the
government spends little on nature. Swedes chose the opposite. Across 2,101
people making repeated choices between funding schemes, they consistently
preferred mandatory approaches, and were willing to pay more for schemes that
governments monitor rather than companies audit themselves. The people who care
most about nature, and who gave their own money when given the chance, were the
most opposed to relying on voluntary contributions. They judged mandatory
schemes more likely to actually work. If governments are hesitating to regulate
because they expect public resistance, this suggests the resistance may not be
there.
