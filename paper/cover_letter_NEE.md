**Cover Letter — Nature Ecology & Evolution**

Dear Editors,

Following your encouraging response to our presubmission enquiry, we submit
"Citizens prefer mandatory over voluntary biodiversity financing even under
favorable conditions," by Nils Droste, Jens Marl Christiansen and Yohei Mitani.

*Nature Ecology & Evolution* has published a sustained body of work on the supply
side of biodiversity finance: that private finance alone will not deliver
conservation goals (Kedward et al. 2023), that policy mandates are needed to
mobilise private restoration finance (Löfqvist et al. 2023), that upscaling
private biodiversity investment in Europe faces structural barriers (zu Ermgassen
et al. 2025), and that nature markets need binding rules to be credible (zu
Ermgassen et al. 2026). These establish why voluntary instruments struggle to
scale. We ask the prior question: do citizens want conservation financed
voluntarily at all?

Using a preregistered discrete choice experiment (n = 2,101) with an incentivised
donation experiment, we find Swedish citizens consistently prefer mandatory
financing. Public monitoring commands a willingness-to-pay premium of roughly USD
160 per household per year (95% CI 127 to 195), and donation-financed programmes
are valued some USD 250 below an otherwise identical tax-financed one. Crucially,
the standard objection fails: strategic free-riding predicts the highest
voluntary preference among high-concern non-donors, and we observe the lowest.
The citizens who care most, and who give their own money under incentive, are the
most opposed to voluntary financing.

The test is demanding by design. Sweden ranks fifth of 91 countries on
interpersonal trust, so citizens have little reason to doubt that others would
contribute, while its public biodiversity spending is among the lowest in Europe,
so the state cannot be seen as already covering the problem. Both leading
alternative explanations are therefore closed off. If citizens anywhere should
favour voluntary channels, it is here.
