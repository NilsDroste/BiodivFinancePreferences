**Cover Letter — Nature Ecology & Evolution**

Dear Editors,

Following your encouraging response to our presubmission enquiry, we are pleased
to submit "Citizens prefer mandatory over voluntary biodiversity financing even
under favorable conditions," by Nils Droste, Jens Marl Christiansen and Yohei
Mitani.

**The gap we address.** *Nature Ecology & Evolution* has published a sustained
body of work on the supply side of biodiversity finance: that heavy reliance on
private finance alone will not deliver conservation goals (Kedward et al. 2023),
that policy mandates are needed to mobilise private restoration finance (Löfqvist
et al. 2023), that upscaling private biodiversity investment in Europe faces
persistent structural barriers (zu Ermgassen et al. 2025), and that existing
nature markets require binding rules to be scientifically credible (zu Ermgassen
et al. 2026). Together these establish why voluntary and market-based instruments
struggle to scale. The prior question has not been asked directly: do citizens
want conservation financed through voluntary channels at all? Our paper supplies
that demand-side complement to a literature this journal has largely defined.

**The design.** We use a most-likely case logic, which requires two conditions to
hold jointly. Voluntary provision must be feasible: Sweden ranks fifth of 91
countries worldwide on interpersonal trust, the mechanism most directly relevant
to voluntary collective action. And there must be an unmet need for voluntary
finance to fill rather than duplicate: Swedish public expenditure on biodiversity
and landscape protection is USD 16 per capita, among the lowest in Europe. Sweden
is, with the partial exception of Austria at substantially lower trust, the only
European country combining top-tercile trust with bottom-tercile public
biodiversity spending. If citizens anywhere should prefer to route conservation
finance voluntarily, it is here.

We should flag one refinement since our enquiry. That abstract described Sweden
as ranking high on per-capita conservation spending. On verification against
Eurostat COFOG data, the opposite is true, and we have rebuilt the case
characterisation accordingly. The design is stronger for it: the two conditions
now close off the two leading alternative explanations for a mandatory
preference, since citizens neither doubt their neighbours will contribute nor can
regard the state as already having the problem covered.

**The findings.** Using a preregistered discrete choice experiment (n = 2,101)
combined with an incentivised donation experiment, we find Swedish citizens
consistently prefer mandatory instruments. Mandatory corporate offsetting carries
the highest mean utility, and public monitoring commands a willingness-to-pay
premium of approximately USD 160 per household per year (95% CI 127 to 195). The
standard political-economy objection, that apparent mandatory preference reflects
strategic free-riding by high-concern non-contributors, is rejected: the cell the
hypothesis predicts should show the highest voluntary preference shows the
lowest. Respondents also rate mandatory instruments more effective at delivering
biodiversity benefits, and that judgement tracks their instrument choices, though
we are careful to present this as post-choice associational validation rather
than evidence of mediation.

**Why this fits your readership.** The finding bears directly on GBF Target 19,
whose reliance on voluntary private finance presupposes a citizen willingness our
data do not find, in the context where it should be easiest to find. We also
identify a tension your readers will recognise: the instrument citizens most
prefer, mandatory corporate offsetting, is the one with the largest evidence base
for ecological failure in practice. We read this not as an endorsement of
offsetting markets as currently operated but as demand for mandatory corporate
accountability, which sharpens rather than softens the governance prescription.

**Declarations.** The study was preregistered before data collection (AsPredicted
#273748 and #287153); we state explicitly which analyses those registrations
cover and which are exploratory. Anonymized microdata and full replication code
are publicly available, and both the manuscript and supplementary information
render from the deposited data. The study was approved by the Swedish Ethical
Review Authority (2025-04420-01) and all participants gave informed consent. The
manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere, all authors approve this
submission, and we declare no competing interests.

We suggest reviewers with expertise in biodiversity finance and governance,
stated preference methods, and the political economy of environmental policy
instruments.
