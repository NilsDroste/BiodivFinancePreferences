**Cover Letter — Nature Ecology & Evolution**

Dear Editors,

Following your encouraging response to our presubmission enquiry, we are pleased
to submit "Citizens prefer mandatory over voluntary biodiversity financing even
under favorable conditions," by Nils Droste, Jens Marl Christiansen and Yohei
Mitani.

**The gap we address.** *Nature Ecology & Evolution* has published a sustained
body of work on the supply side of biodiversity finance: that heavy reliance on
private finance alone will not deliver conservation goals (Kedward et al. 2023),
that policy mandates are needed to mobilise private restoration finance (Löfqvist
et al. 2023), that upscaling private biodiversity investment in Europe faces
persistent structural barriers (zu Ermgassen et al. 2025), and that nature
markets require binding rules to be scientifically credible (zu Ermgassen et al.
2026). Together these establish why voluntary and market-based instruments
struggle to scale. The prior question has not been asked directly: do citizens
want conservation financed through voluntary channels at all? We supply that
demand-side complement to a literature this journal has largely defined.

**What we find.** Using a preregistered discrete choice experiment (n = 2,101)
combined with an incentivised donation experiment, we find that Swedish citizens
consistently prefer mandatory biodiversity financing. Mandatory corporate
offsetting carries the highest mean utility; donation-financed programmes are
valued approximately USD 250 per household per year below an otherwise identical
tax-financed programme; and public monitoring commands a willingness-to-pay
premium of approximately USD 160 per household per year (95% CI 127 to 195). The
ordering is stable across conditional and mixed logit specifications and across
every preregistered subgroup.

The result survives the objection that matters most. A standard political-economy
reading holds that apparent mandatory preference reflects strategic free-riding:
high-concern citizens endorsing compulsion so that others pay. That hypothesis
predicts the highest voluntary preference among high-concern non-donors. We
observe the lowest, with non-overlapping confidence intervals against the
disengaged comparison group. The citizens who care most about biodiversity, and
who give their own money in an incentivised allocation, are the most opposed to
voluntary financing. Respondents also rate mandatory instruments more effective
at delivering biodiversity benefits, and that judgement predicts their instrument
choices, which we present as post-choice associational validation rather than
evidence of mediation.

**The design.** We use a most-likely case logic requiring two conditions to hold
jointly. Voluntary provision must be feasible: Sweden ranks fifth of 91 countries
worldwide on interpersonal trust, the mechanism most directly relevant to
voluntary collective action. And there must be an unmet need for voluntary
finance to fill rather than duplicate: Swedish public expenditure on biodiversity
and landscape protection is USD 16 per capita, among the lowest in Europe. These
two conditions close off the leading alternative explanations for a mandatory
preference, since citizens here neither have reason to doubt that others will
contribute nor can regard the state as already having the problem covered. If
citizens anywhere should prefer to route conservation finance voluntarily, it is
here. (Our presubmission abstract described Sweden as a high per-capita
conservation spender; verification against Eurostat COFOG data showed otherwise,
and the case is characterised accordingly here.)

**Why this fits your readership.** Beyond the direct bearing on GBF Target 19,
whose reliance on voluntary private finance presupposes a citizen willingness our
data do not find, we surface a tension your readers will recognise: the
instrument citizens most prefer, mandatory corporate offsetting, is the one with
the largest evidence base for ecological failure in practice. We read this not as
endorsement of offsetting markets as currently operated but as demand for
mandatory corporate accountability, which sharpens rather than softens the
governance prescription. The strong preference for public monitoring over private
audit points the same way.

**Declarations.** The study was preregistered before data collection (AsPredicted
#273748 and #287153), and we state explicitly which analyses those registrations
cover and which are exploratory. Anonymized microdata and full replication code
are publicly available, and both the manuscript and supplementary information
render from the deposited data without access to restricted files. The study was
approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (2025-04420-01) and all
participants gave informed consent. The manuscript is not under consideration
elsewhere, all authors approve this submission, and we declare no competing
interests.
